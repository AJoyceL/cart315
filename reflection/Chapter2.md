What stood out to me the most in this chapter was the relationship between Pippin Barr— the creator — and the people playing *PONGS*— the players. Barr doesn’t treat the game as something completely finished; instead, he experiments with the rules, creates more variations, watches how people respond, and stays open to changing the game based on their experiences. Both Barr and players go through this experience with an almost child-like wonder to curiosities— enthusiastically. 

My favourite example of this relationship was *Fertility Pong*, where every successful hit duplicates the ball. Because of a mistake in the code, the game could suddenly create hundreds of balls at once, and instead of fixing it, Barr kept it because it made *PONGS* more chaotic and enjoyable. One player even emailed him to say they liked it and suggested raising the winning score so the game could last longer. I find it amazing how such a small and random comment could help shape the variation.

What I like about his approach is to make players feel included in the creative process. They aren’t just playing something he made. Their reactions and suggestions can become part of its development. Barr still decides what to add, but he shows that good ideas can come from everywhere— even mistakes or unexpected player behaviour. 

If I were the player whose suggestion was used, I would feel proud and accomplished. Seeing my idea included would make me feel notices and more connected to the game in the way a child feels sudden pride and satisfaction when their parents and/or teachers frame their drawings. It would almost feel like I had left a small piece of myself in it. Chapter 2 made me realise that listening to users doesn’t just improve a project; it can also make them feel like their opinions and experiences genuinely matter.
